# Research Study Types and Design

Understanding study design is a prerequisite to conducting or analyzing research. Clinical research is broadly divided into **Experimental** and **Observational** studies based on whether there is an intervention.

## 1. Experimental (Interventional) Studies
Does the study involve an intervention allocated by the researcher? If yes:
- **Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT):** Allocation is entirely by chance (random).
  - *Highest level of evidence* among single study designs.
  - Avoids selection and confounding biases.
  - *Intention-to-Treat principle:* Every patient randomized enters primary analysis in their assigned group, regardless of compliance.
  - *Per-Protocol principle:* Identifies treatment effect under optimal conditions (only fully compliant patients).
  - *Limitations:* Expensive, external validity issues (volunteers may not represent general public), unethical for harmful exposures.
- **Non-Randomized Controlled Trial:** There is an intervention, but allocation is NOT by chance.

## 2. Observational Studies
No intervention is allocated by the researcher. Divided into Analytical and Descriptive based on the presence of a comparison group.

### A. Descriptive Studies (No Comparison Group)
- **Case Report:** Details experience with a single patient.
- **Case Series:** Details experience with a group of patients with similar characteristics.
- **Descriptive Survey:** Describing characteristics in a population of interest.

### B. Analytical Studies (Comparison Group Present)
Based on the direction of inquiry relative to time:
- **Cohort Study (Forward looking / Prospective):** From *Exposure to Outcome*.
  - *Strength:* Less recall bias, enables calculation of true incidence rate and relative risk.
  - *Weakness:* Poor for rare diseases or long-latency outcomes; expensive/slow.
- **Case-Control Study (Backward looking / Retrospective):** From *Outcome to Exposure*.
  - *Strength:* Useful for rare outcomes or diseases taking a long time to develop (e.g., cancer). Cheaper/faster.
  - *Weakness:* Recall bias, inappropriate control groups, cannot calculate incidence/relative risk (uses Odds Ratios instead).
- **Cross-Sectional Study (Snapshot):** *Exposure and Outcome* investigated simultaneously.

## 3. Basic vs. Translational vs. Clinical Research
- **Basic Research:** Fundamental theoretical research to advance knowledge (How? What? Why?) without immediate practical application. Uses models to predict hypotheses.
- **Translational Research:** Takes basic research facts and translates them into potential therapeutics.
- **Clinical Research:** Tests those solutions safely in human trials.

## 4. Basic Research Models
- **In silico:** Computational models/simulations (e.g., molecular docking, toxicity prediction). Fast/cheap but demands rigorous validation.
- **In vitro:** Assays outside living organism in controlled environment (e.g., cell cultures, PCR, ELISA, organs-on-a-chip). Inexpensive/high-throughput, but extrapolation to humans can be weak.
- **Ex vivo:** Experiments with living cells/tissues taken from an organism and studied externally.
- **In vivo:** Studying a whole living subject (animal models). Used for understanding physiological processes/systemic interactions. Not always reproducible in humans due to genetic differences.

## 5. Omics Sciences
The study of complete sets of biological molecules:
- **Genomics:** Structure/function/mapping of genomes.
- **Transcriptomics:** All mRNA in a cell/tissue.
- **Proteomics:** Proteins, their structures, quantities, and modifications.
- **Metabolomics:** Profile of all chemical processes involving metabolites.

## Agent Directives
- **MANDATORY:** When extracting data or evaluating primary studies for a meta-analysis, correctly identify and document the specific study design (e.g., RCT vs. Cohort vs. Case-Control).
- **MANDATORY:** Weight the evidence based on the study design hierarchy (RCTs > Cohort > Case-Control > Case Series/Reports).
