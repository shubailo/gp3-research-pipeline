# Introduction to Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (SRMAs)

## 1. Definitions and Distinctions
- **Systematic Review (SR):** A literature review that uses strict, systematic methods to collect data, critically appraise research, and synthesize findings *qualitatively* (narrative summary).
- **Meta-Analysis (MA):** A systematic review where individual studies and their results are analyzed *statistically* to integrate findings quantitatively (mathematical summary).

> [!NOTE]
> **Rule of Thumb:** All meta-analyses are systematic reviews, but NOT all systematic reviews are meta-analyses.

## 2. Level of Evidence
The strength of a meta-analysis depends strictly on the primary studies it includes:
- **Strongest:** MA of Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs).
- **Weaker:** MA containing retrospective cohorts, case-controls, or case reports.
- **Agent Check:** Always assess the study designs of included articles to determine the true level of evidence. Combining 9 RCTs with 1 prospective cohort reduces the overall evidence level to that of a cohort study.

## 3. Advantages of SRMAs
- Synthesizes a vast amount of secondary data quickly.
- Crucial for Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) and formulating clinical guidelines.
- Takes less time to complete compared to primary research (e.g., RCTs or basic research), allowing fast implementation by clinicians.
- Identifies and fills knowledge gaps.

## 4. Limitations and Risks (Bias & Heterogeneity)
- **Quality Dependency:** The phrase "garbage in, garbage out" applies; quality relies on included publications.
- **Heterogeneity ("Mixing Apples and Oranges"):** Risk of neglecting inter-study differences and pooling them inappropriately.
- **Publication Bias:** Negative or less significant results are often unpublished, skewing the meta-analysis.
- **Statistical Expertise:** Requires high statistical rigor to ensure valid results.

## 5. Team and Protocol Requirements
- **Team Effort:** Should include clinical experts, statisticians, methodology experts, and potentially other stakeholders (patients, policymakers).
- **A Priori Protocol:** A protocol MUST be developed and published *before* starting the review.
  - The review question dictates the methodology, not the evidence.
  - Prevents data-driven ad-hoc changes (fishing for "better" results).
  - Enhances transparency, reproducibility, and reduces bias.

## 6. PRISMA Statement
- **PRISMA Checklist:** A mandatory guideline for conducting and reporting SRMAs.
- **PRISMA Flowchart:** A visual representation of the search magnitude and each phase of the study selection process.

## Agent Directives
- **MANDATORY:** Always verify the quality and type of the included primary studies when evaluating a meta-analysis.
- **MANDATORY:** Ensure an a priori protocol exists (or is planned) before beginning data collection to mitigate bias.
- **MANDATORY:** Adhere strictly to the PRISMA guidelines and checklist during the planning and reporting phases.
