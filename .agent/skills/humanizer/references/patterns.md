## 1. Root cause

Models regress to the mean and are tuned toward broadly palatable prose. Specific, unusual facts are statistically rare, so they get replaced with generic, positive descriptions. "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry". The subject becomes less specific and more exaggerated. Each model and version has its own idiolect, so what marks one may not mark another. ChatGPT and Grok lean toward broader context. Gemini and Claude tend to be more concise.

Fix for almost everything below: bring back the concrete detail, or cut the sentence.

## 2. Content patterns

**Undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends.** Puffing up arbitrary details as representing or contributing to a larger topic, even for mundane subjects like etymology or population data.
Words to watch: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, "distinction matters because...".
Variants: hedging preambles that admit low importance and then argue importance anyway. In writing about animals or plants, stressing ties to the wider ecosystem and conservation status even when unknown. Hedging disclaimers followed by editorializing about why a nuance matters.

**Claims that a topic has influenced discussions.** "Has shaped conversations/discussions/debate about...", often with a rule of three: "has started discussions about X, Y, and Z". Also situating the subject in broader "debates" or "public discussions".

**Undue emphasis on notability, attribution and media coverage.** Proving importance by listing outlets ("featured in major national and regional media"), naming source types (trade publications, regional media), echoing guideline wording like "independent coverage", and attributing the model's own shallow analysis to a named source.
Words to watch: independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in.

**Undue emphasis on identification by primary or tertiary sources.** Stilted "records say" phrasing: "His personal website identifies him as...", "His parliamentary biography records that...", "is listed in", "his professional profile lists". Common since 2026.

**Undue emphasis on online presence.** "Maintains an active/strong digital presence", "social media presence". Rare in human text before about 2024.

**Superficial analyses.** A present-participle clause tacked on the end of a sentence to assert significance, recognition or impact, sometimes pinned to a named source that never said it.
Words to watch: highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing..., ensuring..., reflecting/symbolizing..., contributing to..., cultivating/fostering..., encompassing..., enhancing..., valuable insights, align/resonate with.

**Vague expression of connection or association.** Instead of "In 2017 John Doe was CEO of ExampleCorp", the text says he was "associated with leadership of ExampleCorp". Words to watch: in connection with/to, connected with/to, in association with, associated with. Often paired with "particularly/widely associated".

**Vague attributions and overgeneralized opinions.** Opinions pinned on an unnamed authority, the number of sources exaggerated, one or two views presented as widely held, "multiple reviewers" when one is cited, lists implied to be non-exhaustive with no basis.
Words to watch: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, several sources/publications (when only a few are cited), "such as" before an exhaustive list. (Wikipedia flags this section as dated, but it is still worth avoiding.)

**Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects.** "Despite its [positive words], X faces several challenges...", "Despite these challenges...", "Challenges and Legacy", "Future Outlook", ending on a vague upbeat note or speculation about initiatives. The tell is the rigid formula, not the mere mention of challenges.

**"Awards and recognition" and "X and Y" headings.** Section headers in the format "X and Y" are common, and "Awards and recognition" is nearly ubiquitous in AI output.

**Hedging disclaimers about source availability and usage.** Drawing attention to gaps, or cautioning the reader.
Words to watch: While specific details are limited/scarce..., not widely available/documented/disclosed, ...in the provided/available sources/search results..., [claim] should be treated/presented as... rather than..., based on available information, ...does not by itself establish...
Mid-2026 patterns: negative parallelism with "rather than" warning that a claim is speculative or symbolic; "the available information does not establish..."; "should not be confused with...". When the model cannot find information about a person it often says they "maintain a low profile" or "keep personal details private", which is speculation.
Older (about 2023) didactic disclaimers: "it's important to note", advice to an imagined reader on safety or controversy, jurisdiction caveats.

## 3. Language and grammar

**Avoidance of basic copulatives.** "Serves as", "stands as", "marks", "functions as", "operates as", "represents", "boasts", "features", "maintains", "offers" in place of "is", "are", "has". Also "refers to" in a lead sentence, as if the subject were a word. One study found a drop of over 10% in "is" and "are" in academic writing in 2023, and AI copyedits "improve" text this way. Elaborate versions: "ventured into politics as a candidate" instead of "was a candidate", "began his career as" instead of "was".

**Negative parallelisms.**
- Not just X, but also Y: "Not only ... but ...", "It is not just ..., it's ...", "not only from A, but also from B". Also across two sentences ("He hailed from the esteemed Duse family... Eugenio's life, however, took a path that...").
- Not X, but Y: "It's not ..., it's ...", "no ..., no ..., just ...".
- Y rather than X: the reversed form, common in recent output ("prioritizing consolidation of power rather than ideological purity", "a bridge ... rather than a replacement").
Human writers use these too, especially in myth-busting pieces. The problem is using them as a reflex, to retroactively correct a misconception nobody held.

**Leads treating list titles as proper nouns.** The opening sentence defines a generic article title as if it were a named entity ("Historic churches of Lamont County refers to...").

**Rule of three.** From "adjective, adjective, adjective" to "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase". Used to make shallow analysis look comprehensive. Stronger in places people rarely bother with flourishes, such as short summaries.

## 4. AI vocabulary by era

A high density of these words after 2022 is one of the strongest tells. They co-occur. One or two can be coincidence. The words listed are overused, and the page says synonyms are not necessarily overused, so do not simply swap in a thesaurus word. Context matters (a literal underscore character is fine).

Core words to watch: additionally (especially opening a sentence), align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate/intricacies, landscape (abstract noun), meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (abstract noun), testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant.

- 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant.
- Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
- Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus the notability and media-coverage phrasing.
- Mid-2026 on (GPT-6): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally. Also more periods and commas and shorter sentences held to a strict 8 to 20 word band.
- Grok: overuses pseudo-scientific words such as causal, empirical, correlate, and still overuses underscore.

## 5. Style and formatting

- Title case in headings instead of sentence case.
- Overuse of boldface in prose, sometimes with literal asterisks.
- Inline-header vertical lists ("**Term**: explanation").
- Wrong heading levels, such as a level-1 title at the top and multiple level-1 headings.
- Emoji at the start of headings or list entries.
- Curly quotation marks and apostrophes instead of straight ones. Typical of ChatGPT and DeepSeek. Gemini and Claude usually use straight ones. Curly quotes are normal in typeset documents, so only treat them as a tell in plain-text contexts.
- Em dash overuse (see section 10 for the history). Em dashes often have spaces around them, and are used where a comma, parentheses, colon, hyphen or en dash would be more natural.
- Markdown symbols in the wrong place: asterisks for bold, number signs for headings, "---" dividers, [text](url) links where the destination doesn't render them, code fences around plain text.
- Canned user-page style: "Welcome To My Page!", "About Me", "My Interests", "Let's Connect!", emoji and bold.

## 6. Communication meant for the user

Collaborative phrasing that ended up in the deliverable. Words to watch: a Wikipedia-style article, I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right!, Would you like..., is there anything else, let me know, more detailed breakdown, here is a..., check/verify/confirm... before publishing.
Also: hidden notes to the user in comments, prewriting advice ("Before creating a real page, your channel should have..."), reviewer notes and "submission statements" arguing that the text meets guidelines, AI-use disclosures stuffed into the text.

**Phrasal templates and placeholders.** Mad-Libs style fill-ins that were never filled: [Entertainer's Name], (Add your channel URL here), "Evidence to be supplied", "Add if available with citation", placeholder dates like 2026-xx-xx, placeholder URLs like example.com/source1 or INSERT_SOURCE_URL.

**Prompt refusals and cutoff statements (older).** "As an AI language model...", "I cannot offer medical advice, but I can...", "As of my last knowledge update...".

## 7. Citations and links

Wikipedia-specific in form, but the principles carry over to anything that cites.
- Broken external links in bulk, invalid DOIs or ISBNs, DOIs that resolve to unrelated papers, made-up articles with real-looking details.
- Book citations without page numbers, or with pages that do not support the claim.
- Tracking parameters left in URLs: utm_source=chatgpt.com, utm_source=openai, utm_source=copilot.com, referrer=grok.com.
- Odd reference artifacts: the ↩ character around footnotes, leaked markup like contentReference[oaicite:1], unused or undefined named references.
- Outdated access dates (older models).

## 8. Comments, edit summaries and change notes

Comments: misquoting policies or citing shortcuts that do not exist, long comments split into titled sections, downplaying AI use, urging critics to fix content instead of asking about origin, and a customer-service voice ("carefully reviewed", apologizing for any inconvenience, "please point them out to me and I will try to resolve them").

Edit summaries (also applies to commit messages and changelogs):
- Rigid formulas and first-person explanations in older output, exhaustively procedural ones in newer.
- Canned assurance of rule compliance: ensured, adheres to, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved, in compliance with, guidelines/style/standards, neutrality, neutral tone, encyclopedic tone, clarity, flow.
- Mentions of what was preserved, retained or avoided: "while preserving the original meaning".
- Overemphasis on citations: "added sourced information", "improved attribution", "with independent sources".
- Itemizing parameter or template names and markup.
- Stating the obvious reason for the edit ("addressed reviewer feedback").
- Rule of three in a place where nobody would bother.
Humans write "Added info about the artist's debut" or "ce".

## 9. Human-writing signals and ineffective indicators

Signs of human writing the page lists: text from before 30 November 2022; ability to explain one's choices; and constructions AI avoids because it aims for a formal, neutral tone:
- Simple is/has phrases (there is a, it has a).
- Plain words over stiff synonyms: wrote vs authored, moved vs relocated, used vs utilized, tried vs attempted, died vs passed away.
- Superlative or definitive statements (one of the best, is the only, was the first).
- Qualifiers and intensifiers (very, perhaps, tends to).
- Wordy fragments (as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, the fact that).
Also: a sudden shift in style relative to a person's earlier writing, and style that stays consistent with pre-2022 writing.

Ineffective or misleading indicators (do not treat these as problems, and do not degrade writing to avoid them): perfect grammar, mixed casual and formal registers, "bland" or "robotic" prose, fancy or academic prose in general, transition words in isolation, unsourced content, bizarre wikitext, correct wikitext.

## 10. Historical indicators

Common in older models, less in newer ones, but still found:
- **Promotional language (2023 to 2025):** boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array. Press-release or travel-guide tone. Faded from about 2024.
- **Knowledge-cutoff statements (2022 to 2023).**
- **Section summaries:** "In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall", conclusion sections, and paragraphs that end by restating the idea.
- **Prompt refusals.**
- **Em dash overuse (2022 to September 2026).** Formulaic, punched-up, sales-like, usually spaced. Most useful in combination with other signs. GPT-5.1 and later were tuned to suppress them. A July 2026 study found that among contemporary models only Claude used em dashes more than professional writers. Models now hard-coded to avoid them lose punctuation variety, using only commas and periods, and that flatness has become its own sign.
- **Abrupt cut-offs** from token limits.
- **Lexical diversity and elegant variation:** repetition penalties led to synonym cycling (the artists, the non-conformists, the creatives). Non-native writers may also avoid repeats, so this is not decisive.

## 11. Rewrite examples

Before: "Nestled in the heart of the city, the café stands as a vibrant testament to the area's rich culinary tapestry, offering warm hospitality, exceptional coffee, and unforgettable ambiance."
After: write what is known: the street, when it opened, what it roasts. If you have none of that, ask the user.

Before: "It's not just a tool, it's a catalyst for innovation. Whether you're a beginner or an expert, you'll find it intuitive, powerful, and flexible."
After: "The tool handles setup for you. Experts can switch the defaults off."

Before: "The festival has shaped conversations about heritage, identity, and community, highlighting its enduring significance."
After: "About 4,000 people came last year. Most were from the surrounding villages." (Only if true. Otherwise cut.)

Before: "While specific details about his early life are limited, available sources suggest he maintains a low profile."
After: cut. State what is known, and say nothing about what is not.

Before: "Great question! Here's a comprehensive overview. In summary, effective communication is crucial. I hope this helps!"
After: start with the answer, end on the last useful point.

Before (commit note): "Refined the parser to ensure improved clarity and compliance while preserving existing behavior."
After: "Parser no longer drops the last line when the file has no trailing newline."
